# The Boardroom: Who Is Actually Reading This

When the audience is a board of directors, Pass 1 (audience and purpose) needs
more than "who are they" — it needs how they actually read, decide, and
exercise power. This reference is based on [What does a board of
directors do?](https://www.anildash.com/2024/06/20/dash-board/) by Anil Dash.

## How board members read your document

- **The pre-brief is the real document.** The meeting is where the board
  reacts; the pre-brief is where they form their view. If board members are
  surprised by anything at the meeting, that reads as poor preparation, not as
  news.
- **They skim for three things:** surprises, asks, and numbers that moved.
  Everything else is context they'll reconstruct only if they need to.
- **They read to form a position, not to decide.** In a well-functioning
  organization, the executive team decides and the board supports. Your
  document's job is to make the decision the team wants to make look like the
  obvious, well-supported choice.
- **Bad news ages badly.** A problem disclosed at the meeting is a trust
  event. The same problem disclosed in the pre-brief is just management.

## How power actually works in the room

- **Investor members have an explicit agenda** (returns) and won't hide it.
  Other members have quieter agendas — career, reputation, mission, or a
  personal stake in a specific outcome.
- **Boards are biased toward the status quo.** They exist to stabilize, not to
  shock. You are not trying to jolt them into radical change; you are trying
  to make an evolutionary change look possible and safe.
- **Information reaches them filtered through the executives they oversee.**
  That filter is why "why didn't the board know?" is the most common board
  failure. If your document is the least-filtered thing they read, it carries
  more weight than you think.
- **Back-channel matters more than the room.** Decisions that look like they're
  made at the meeting are usually formed in one-on-one pre-briefs beforehand.
  A document worth forwarding in a private email is doing half its work.

## What boards punish

- Surprises at the meeting, especially financial ones
- Buried bad news, or bad news without a plan
- Unanswerable questions ("How do we improve?")
- Asks without a deadline, or deadlines without a reason
- Documents that read like they were written for the author

## What boards reward

- Bad news early, with a plan and an owner
- Options with a recommendation — never a bare list of choices
- Numbers with a comparison: vs. target, vs. last quarter, vs. plan
- Specific asks with dates: "Approve X by November 1"
- Measuring the right things — and naming what can't be measured at all

## The realistic goal

The goal of a board document is rarely "the board agrees with me." It's
usually one of three things:

1. **The board approves the specific ask** by the stated date.
2. **The board asks leadership about X** — which is how issues get on the
   agenda. Making enough noise that the board has to ask is a legitimate
   strategy, and the document is the noise.
3. **The board doesn't intervene.** For a team executing well, "no surprises,
   no questions" is the win, and the document's job is to be unremarkable in
   the best sense.

Write for whichever of these is actually the goal. A document that aims for
approval but only achieves "the board asks questions" has failed — and vice
versa.
