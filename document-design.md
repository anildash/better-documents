# Document Design Guidelines

*Distilled from [Make better documents.](https://www.anildash.com/2024/03/10/make-better-documents/) by Anil Dash. Original post © Anil Dash.*

---

## 1. Know your audience and your goals

Before writing a single word, answer these:

- **Who is reading this?** What's their day like? What else is competing for their attention?
- **What do they need from this document?** What question are they trying to answer?
- **What context are you assuming they have?** Is your framing culturally and historically appropriate for them?
- **Are you asking for something?** If so, make it unmistakably clear. Lay out options with explicit pros and cons.
- **Is a decision required?** Name the stakeholders, surface the counter-arguments, and state the timeline — and explain *why* that timeline matters to *them*, not just to you.

> The most common failure: starting from deep inside your own head rather than from your reader's perspective. Don't open with a recitation of your own anxieties or insecurities.

**The right structure:** Start from common ground → name the shared goal → acknowledge where you already are on that path → identify what remains → close by asking for their help.

---

## 2. Stop over-formatting

Most formatting is noise. Default to restraint.

- **Bold**: use sparingly, for genuinely important things.
- **Italics**: use rarely, for emphasis.
- **Underline**: don't. It signals a clickable link and confuses readers.
- **Color**: pick one (maybe two, max). Use shades if needed. Apply across the *entire* document, including charts.
- **Fonts**: one, or two at most.
- **Borders**: remove them. Use white space instead (see below).

Combining bold + italic + underline + color on the same text communicates only one thing: you don't know what's important.

---

## 3. Use white space

White space does the heavy lifting that over-formatting tries (and fails) to do.

- Give content room to breathe. Increase margins and spacing around headings and sections.
- Use **bullet points** to make content skimmable, force concision, and reveal whether items actually belong in the same category.
- **Avoid filler images.** A blank space is better than a stock photo or clip art that has no specific relevance to your point. Visual noise costs the reader attention.

---

## 4. Learn and use your tools consistently

- Use **styles** (heading levels, paragraph styles) so spacing and formatting are applied uniformly, not manually.
- Inconsistent formatting — different fonts, sizes, or colors across slides or sections — signals to readers that there's a *reason* for the change. They'll spend time looking for meaning that isn't there.
- When pulling in content from other sources (copied slides, embedded charts), do a formatting pass to make everything consistent before sharing.

---

## 5. Sequence by importance, not by creation order

- Front-load key points. Don't make readers dig.
- Within a page or slide, most important information goes **at the top**.
- If you're *not* ordering by importance (e.g., chronologically or by category), **say so explicitly** in the document — otherwise half your audience will be lost.

---

## 6. State your conclusion first

This is not a murder mystery. Don't build to a dramatic reveal.

- Open with the key conclusion, request, or question.
- Then walk through the supporting logic and context.
- Restate the conclusion at the end to drive toward your goal.

**Critical:** Put the important points *on the page*. Do not plan to "speak to" the most vital information verbally alongside the document. If a key stakeholder is absent or distracted, they'll read the document and miss your point entirely. Don't bury central conclusions in speaker notes or appendices.

---

## 7. Give people wayfinding

Help readers know where they are in the story.

- A brief outline or progress indicator at the start (or recurring throughout a long document) reduces cognitive load.
- If you have distinct sections, assign them a color from your limited palette and use it *consistently* — this builds a mental map.
- **Summarize data in chart/table titles.** Don't make readers interpret a chart cold. A short "here's what this shows" in the title sets the right frame before they engage with the numbers.

---

## 8. Ask answerable questions

If your document drives toward a decision, frame questions so they can actually be answered.

- ❌ "How can we do better?" — philosophical, not actionable
- ✅ "Do we go with Option A (higher cost, faster) or Option B (lower cost, slower)?" — a choice with defined tradeoffs

Constrained choices produce constructive responses. Open-ended questions produce paralysis or deflection.

---

## 9. Close by restating mission and shared goals

End by reminding your audience of alignment — on purpose, on values, on what you're collectively working toward.

- If you're persuading: it gives them psychological permission to trust your intent.
- If you're deciding: it gives them confidence to move forward.
- If you're informing: it invites clarifying questions from a place of shared investment.

---

## 10. Name files and documents well

The title of a document is itself a critical piece of information.

- **Think about how your audience will search for it later.** If they're at another org, they'll search by your name/org — include it. If you're on the same team, include the project and goal.
- **Include the date and topic in the filename.** This helps recipients know if they have the latest version without opening it.
- **For versions:** dates in the filename beat sequential numbers. `_final_final` is not a versioning strategy.
- **For meetings:** name the invite after the purpose of the conversation, not after the participants. "Meeting with Sam" is useless on Sam's calendar.

---

## Quick reference

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Use white space to separate content | Add borders to tables and sections |
| State your conclusion at the top | Build to a reveal |
| Summarize charts in their titles | Make readers interpret data cold |
| Use one consistent color palette | Apply multiple colors for variety |
| Put key decisions in the document | Plan to "speak to" them verbally |
| Name files with date + topic + context | Use `v2_final_FINAL` |
| Use bullets to force concision | Use dense prose where lists work better |
| Apply heading styles for consistency | Manually format titles on each slide |
